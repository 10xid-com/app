The short answer

What is being built, and what changed along the way

A portal both sides log into. Clients see their own company's jobs; staff see every client. Work moves in both directions through the same machinery. It runs on several domains, all of them windows onto one database — never a copy.

Four things changed while this was being planned, and each is worth stating plainly because each reverses something that was previously assumed.

  1. Clerk is out, and not on cost. The earlier record in this repo rejected Clerk because organizations cost $100/month. That turned out to be wrong — organizations are free on every Clerk plan. Clerk is out for two different reasons: it charges $10/month per client domain, which scales with exactly the thing this product sells, and it cannot express different session lengths for staff and clients, which is a requirement you called non-negotiable.
  2. The login is ours. Written here, in this repo, against a published security standard rather than a vendor's roadmap. No per-domain fee, no third-party DNS records, no account to lose.
  3. The portal is separate from the Rotary storefront. Those 68 accounts stay in Supabase, untouched. Nothing built here can break a live sign-in.
  4. Everyone gets cross-domain sign-in, not just staff. Considered narrowing it to staff — the evidence supported that — and you chose the full version. It is built for everyone.

What is actually running today

The first question asked was whether rotary.brandingcentres.com already has real accounts, because that decides migration versus greenfield. It does — and there is a second live system nobody mentioned.

172 distinct people across the two live systems, and only 2 hold an account in both. Every account is email-and-password with bcrypt hashes; there are no Google or social sign-ins to carry across.

The important distinction: those 68 Rotary accounts are shoppers, not job-exchange clients. The database is a shop. A portal login would not replace that login, which is why the two stay separate.

Consequence

A Rotary person may end up with two logins on the same domain family. That is a real cost of keeping them separate, accepted deliberately. If it ever needs undoing, bcrypt hashes can be imported into a new system without forcing anyone to reset a password — so the door stays open without building anything now.

What the existing codebase gets right

RotaryHomepage is a Next.js 15 app on Railway with Supabase behind it. Two things in it are worth copying outright:

  • The role is read off the account record after authenticating, never taken from the sign-in form. requireAdmin() loads profiles.is_admin for the signed-in user and redirects otherwise. That is the correct shape and the portal keeps it.
  • The theme is tokenised. rotary.blue, rotary.gold, rotary.ivory, rotary.ink, a card shadow scale, four display sizes, Playfair over Inter. A real design system, and the portal inherits its shape.

And the two things it does that the portal must not

Pattern to break

Staff data access runs on the service key, which switches the database's own rules off completely. 35 of the 36 admin API routes remember to call requireAdmin() first — genuinely good discipline — but it is a check copy-pasted 35 times, and route 37 is the one that forgets. The audit log records that an admin acted, not which customer's data was opened or why.

The data rules are per person, not per company. Every policy reads auth.uid() = user_id. A portal needs a Rotary person to see a job their colleague raised, because the job belongs to Rotary — so the whole shape changes from "my rows" to "my company's rows".

Decisions already settled

Each one made deliberately, and reversible at a stated cost

01

The portal is separate from the storefront

No migration. The 174 live accounts stay where they are and nothing built here can break them.

02

Everyone gets cross-domain sign-in

Not just staff. Sign in once, land signed in on any of the portal's domains, no second prompt.

03

The login is built here, not bought

Our cookie, our session table, our domains. No per-domain fee, no vendor DNS records, no third-party outage that logs out every client at once.

04

A session lasts until you sign out

Both clocks are off. An expiry nobody asked for is indistinguishable from the thing being broken, and it rebuilds the "can you let me back in" burden the portal exists to remove. What bounds a staff session is not time but reach: one client at a time, through a grant that carries a reason and lapses in 30 minutes.

05

No passwords — a six-digit code by email

Clients sign in a handful of times a year. A forgotten password recreates the support burden the portal exists to remove, and there is no password database to leak. Staff get the code plus a second factor.

06

The hostname decides branding, never permission

Which company's data a person can read comes from their session. The address bar carries no authority whatsoever.

07

Sessions are checked against the database on every request

Clerk's design trusts a signed token for up to 60 seconds to avoid a database read. That buys speed you do not need at 200 users and costs a 60-second window where a sacked employee is still signed in. Recommended, not yet locked — say the word and it is built Clerk's way.

08

Safari is not in the test matrix

Your call, and self-hosting happens to remove the risk that made it matter — see below.

How one sign-in reaches several domains

The part that has to be right, in plain language

A browser will not let a cookie cross from one domain to another. That is a browser rule, not a setting, and every workaround that used to exist has been closed: third-party cookies are gone in Safari and partitioned in Firefox, partitioned cookies cannot be shared across sites by design, and the one browser feature that granted this automatically was removed from Chrome on 8 September 2026.

So there is exactly one mechanism left that works in every browser: send the browser to the login host and let it send them back. This is what Clerk does internally, and it is the same shape as the sign-in flows behind every "continue with" button on the web.

1

rotary.brandingcentres.com Someone opens a portal page. The server finds no session cookie for this host.

2

→ login.10xid.com Their browser is redirected to the login host, carrying an identifier for which site sent them — not a web address.

3

login.10xid.com The login host reads its own cookie. Already signed in? Straight to step 4. If not, they sign in here — the only place sign-in ever happens.

4

→ back to the client site The login host mints a single-use ticket, valid 30 seconds, usable only by that one site, and sends the browser back carrying it in a form post rather than in the address bar.

5

rotary.brandingcentres.com That site's server redeems the ticket, confirms who it belongs to, and sets its own cookie on its own domain. The ticket is burned. From here on there are no redirects at all.

What the person sees: the address bar flickers through one hop and lands on the page they asked for, signed in. If they were already signed in at the login host there is no form, no prompt, no typing.

Why the ticket is built the way it is

This flow is not being invented. It is the OAuth authorization-code exchange, and its failure modes are documented in a published security standard, so each rule below answers a known attack rather than a guess.

PropertyWhy
32 random bytes, not a signed tokenNothing to forge and nothing readable inside it. It is only a lookup key.
Stored hashed in PostgresA database leak yields no usable tickets.
Valid ~30 secondsLong enough for a redirect, too short to pass around.
Redeemed once, by an atomic updateTwo simultaneous attempts, one winner. Replay is impossible rather than unlikely.
Bound to one destination hostA ticket minted for one client site is worthless at another.
Delivered by form POSTKeeps it out of browser history, server logs and referrer headers.
Tied to the login session that minted itSigning out at the login host kills tickets in flight.
The trap this design removes

The return destination is an identifier that indexes a table of known domains — never a web address. The two most recent real-world breaks of this exact pattern were both open redirects, where an attacker found a string the server's address parser and the browser's address parser disagreed about. If no address is ever accepted, that entire class of attack cannot occur. Note that the existing storefront does take a raw path in /auth/login?redirect=….

The Safari problem we no longer have

Hosted auth vendors ask you to point clerk.yourdomain.com at their servers. Safari treats a subdomain that resolves to someone else's domain as tracking, and caps that cookie at 7 days — so a 30-day session quietly becomes a 7-day one on every iPhone. Clerk works around it with an extra redirect hop, documented only in their source code.

Our cookie is set by our own server on our own host, with nothing pointing anywhere else. It is first-party by construction, so the cap never applies. Dropping Safari from the test matrix costs much less than it would have.

Sessions: until you sign out

And what bounds them instead of a clock

Every session row carries two expiry times, both enforced on the server: an idle clock that restarts on every visit, and an absolute cap that renewal can never push past. Both are switched off. A session ends when it is signed out, and not before.

RoleIdle timeoutAbsolute capSecond factor
Client——No
Staff——Yes

This was a deliberate reversal. Staff previously expired after 8 hours, or 30 minutes idle, on the reasoning that a staff session reaches every client. In practice an expiry nobody asked for is indistinguishable from the thing being broken, and it rebuilds exactly the "can you let me back in" burden the portal exists to remove.

What replaces the clock — and what does not

Stated plainly: a stolen session cookie now works until somebody notices and ends it, where a staff one previously died within 8 hours on its own. Three things still bound it, and none of them is time.

Staff reach one client at a time, through a grant that carries a typed reason and lapses after 30 minutes. An endless session does not become endless access to every client — that clock is deliberately untouched, because reaching a particular client's data is the privilege, and staying signed in is only convenience. Sessions live server-side, so revoking one from the Sessions screen takes effect on its very next request rather than whenever a token would have expired. And staff still pass a second factor to establish a session at all.

Both values are still copied onto the session row when it is created. Promote someone to staff tomorrow and their existing session does not retroactively change — it keeps the limits it was born with. Which also means a policy change like this one reaches a session only when it is next created: one more sign-in, then not again.

  • The cookie holds a random value and nothing else. It is a lookup key, never a claim.
  • Only the hash of that value is stored, so reading the database yields no live sessions.
  • Liveness is decided from the database row, never from the cookie's own expiry — a browser can keep sending an expired cookie forever.
  • The session identifier is rotated on sign-in and on every privilege change.
  • A "your active sessions — sign out other devices" screen ships in Phase 1. It is the cheapest incident response tool there is.
"Until you sign out" means 400 days, honestly

Chrome and Firefox both hard-cap every cookie at 400 days regardless of what the server asks for, so a session that literally never expires is not achievable in any current browser. The session row is therefore stamped with that same 400 days rather than with some longer number: the row and the cookie lapse together, instead of the row outliving a cookie the browser has already stopped sending.

In practice that is "until you sign out" for anyone using the portal more than once a year — and if a session ever does reach the ceiling, the next sign-in is one six-digit code.

The one rule, enforced twice

Every read and write scoped to the signed-in person's company

One shared helper takes the session and returns a query that is already scoped. Nothing else may touch the database. That is the rule as you wrote it — and it needs a second net underneath, for a specific and unobvious reason.

The leak a shared helper does not catch

Asking the database for "this job and its messages" applies the filter to the job but not automatically to the messages. Each nested part of the query takes its own filter. A helper that scopes the top level will happily pull another company's rows in through a nested relation, and it will look correct in review.

So both, deliberately:

LayerCatches
The shared helper
one module, exported functions that take the session first		 Everyday work. It is what the code reads like, and what makes the rule obvious rather than remembered.
Postgres row-level security
a rule the database enforces itself		 The nested query above, the forgotten filter, and anything a future route gets wrong. The database refuses to return the row at all.

Three traps, and how each is closed

  • The rules silently do nothing if the app connects as the database owner. Teams ship this, write an isolation test, watch it pass, and have no protection — because the test connected as the owner too. Our app and our tests both connect as a restricted role.
  • A pooled connection can leak one request's company setting into the next. This fails open — the next person sees the previous company's rows. It is closed by setting the value transaction-locally, inside a transaction, every time, with no exceptions.
  • A new table added later has no rules until someone remembers. A build check asserts that every table carrying a company id has row-level security switched on and at least one policy. Forgetting fails the build.

Staff are not an exception to this. They do not get ambient access to everything; they exchange their identity for a time-boxed grant to one client, carrying a typed reason. Every query in the application then reads identically for staff and clients alike, and the audit line says which client was opened and why — not merely that an admin was active.

Four decisions inside the model worth challenging

  1. Every child table carries its own copy of the company id. Deliberately redundant, so the shared helper and the database rules apply one identical filter to every table with no joins. This is what makes the rule enforceable rather than aspirational.
  2. Addresses carry an unguessable id; people see ROT-0042. The readable reference is per-client sequential, which would leak how many jobs a client has — so it never appears in an address. Time-ordered random ids keep the database index healthy while being impossible to walk.
  3. The archive is a timestamp, not a second table. A separate archive table is a second place to forget the rule.
  4. Session caps are copied onto the session at creation. Otherwise a later promotion silently lengthens a session already in flight.

Reported honestly or not at all

Safari and Safari Private are excluded by your decision, so no Safari result will be claimed — a Linux container cannot run real Safari, and the nearest engine available is not the same thing where it matters. Sign-out timing will be a measured number, not the theoretical one. If a browser behaves differently from the others, that goes in the report rather than being averaged away.

One practical note: your existing middleware blocks headless browsers by user-agent, so the automated tests need an explicit exemption or they will be answered with 403 before they reach a single page.

Deliberately not in Phase 1

File upload and virus scanning, notification email, per-client theming, archive search, and the automation API with its own service keys. All designed for in the model above, none built yet.

Risks, stated before starting

None of these block a go-ahead; all of them are cheaper to know now

Writing a login is the highest-consequence code in the project

A subtle bug here is a breach, not a nuisance. Mitigations: the handoff follows a published standard rather than my judgement; sessions, hashing and randomness use audited primitives rather than hand-written cryptography; the isolation test runs in the build; and the database enforces the company rule independently of the application code.

Backups protect nothing retroactively

Railway's point-in-time recovery only covers the period after it is switched on, and neither backup mechanism is on by default. Both are enabled before the first real row exists, and a restore is rehearsed once rather than first attempted during an incident.

A documentation-versus-package mismatch in the database tooling

Drizzle's website currently documents a release candidate while the default install is an older version with a different migration layout. Following the docs against the default install produces broken instructions. Exact versions get pinned, and the pinned numbers are recorded in the repo.

Every client domain is a place someone can sign in

More domains means more surface. The mitigations are that sign-in only ever happens at the login host, that each site's cookie is confined to that site, that destinations come from a table rather than a URL, and that any domain must be registered before it can take part at all.

Two logins on one domain family, by choice

A Rotary person may hold a storefront account and a portal account. Accepted deliberately. Hostnames should make it obvious which is which, and the import path stays available if it ever needs merging.
